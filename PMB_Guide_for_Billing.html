What a PMB is

PMBs are the minimum benefits every registered medical scheme must fund, on every option, under the Medical Schemes Act 131 of 1998 and its Regulations (Annexure A). They cover three groups:

  • Emergency medical conditions: any condition that fits the legal definition below, whatever the diagnosis.
  • Diagnosis and Treatment Pairs (DTPs): 271 listed conditions, each paired with the treatment the scheme must pay for. You will often hear "270"; the CMS count is 271.
  • Chronic Disease List (CDL): 26 chronic conditions where the scheme must pay for diagnosis, medication and ongoing management according to the treatment algorithms.
The billing point: PMB status is decided by the diagnosis. If the ICD-10 code on the claim does not show a PMB condition, the scheme's system will not treat it as one. How the patient got ill or injured does not matter, only what the diagnosis is.

Important rules

Paid from risk

PMBs and CDL treatment must be paid from the scheme's risk pool. A scheme may not pay a PMB from the member's medical savings account (Regulation 10(6)).

GEMS options pay PMBs from day-to-day benefits first and then from risk once those run out. GEMS day-to-day benefits are risk-funded, not a savings account.

DSPs and formularies

Schemes may require members to use a Designated Service Provider (DSP), a network or a state facility, and a medicine formulary. If a member voluntarily goes elsewhere or uses a non-formulary medicine, the scheme may pay only up to its DSP rate and charge a co-payment.

If the use is involuntary (an emergency, the DSP was unavailable, or the DSP could not treat the patient in time), the scheme must pay in full, with no co-payment.

Registration and authorisation

CDL conditions must be registered with the scheme. Planned admissions need pre-authorisation. For emergency admissions, obtain authorisation as soon as possible after admission.

GEMS: authorise within one working day of an emergency admission, or a R1,000 co-payment applies per admission.

Emergency medical conditions

Legal definition and how to use itRegulation 7

An emergency medical condition is "the sudden and, at the time, unexpected onset of a health condition that requires immediate medical or surgical treatment, where failure to provide medical or surgical treatment would result in serious impairment to bodily functions or serious dysfunction of a bodily organ or part, or would place the person's life in serious jeopardy."

  • An emergency is a PMB even when the final diagnosis is not on the DTP list.
  • An emergency is involuntary use of a non-DSP: the patient could not choose where to go. The scheme may not apply non-network penalties to the emergency treatment.
  • Once the patient is stable, the scheme may ask for transfer to its DSP. Care after a refused transfer can be treated as voluntary.
For HAE casualty and GEMS: HAE is not on the GEMS hospital network. When a GEMS admission came through casualty as a real emergency, the file must show it: triage category, vital signs, time of arrival and the doctor's note. Obtain auth within one working day and code the diagnosis precisely. If the claim is then rejected under the non-network rule or the R15,000 exclusion, dispute it as an emergency PMB.

In hospital: a CDL condition on its own does not make an admission a PMB. The admission is a PMB when the reason for it is a PMB, for example a hypertensive crisis or acute heart failure. A controlled chronic condition (e.g. I10) should be a secondary code.

Billing checklist for every PMB account

Before the claim goes outDaily
  1. The primary ICD-10 code is the diagnosis the doctor actually made, at full specificity (4th character where it exists), and the notes support it.
  2. The diagnosis is coded, not the symptom. Use an R-code only when no diagnosis was made.
  3. Every injury code has an External Cause Code.
  4. Emergency admissions have triage and vital signs on file, and auth was obtained within one working day (GEMS: R1,000 co-payment otherwise).
  5. Planned admissions have pre-authorisation, and the billed level of care matches what was authorised.
  6. Psychiatric admissions: PMB days used this year are recorded, and an extension is requested before day 21.
  7. Chronic conditions are listed as secondary codes, and the patient's CDL registration is noted.
When a PMB claim is short-paid or rejectedDispute route
  1. Check the rejection code on the remittance. Was it coding, authorisation, benefits exhausted, or non-network/DSP?
  2. If it is a coding error, correct it and resubmit.
  3. If it was paid from savings, or rejected as "benefits exhausted" although it is a PMB, write to the scheme asking for it to be reprocessed as a PMB from risk. Quote the ICD-10 code and Regulation 10(6).
  4. If it was an emergency rejected for non-network use, send the triage notes and argue involuntary use of a non-DSP (Regulations 7 and 8).
  5. If the scheme's internal dispute process fails, the member (or HAE on the member's behalf) can complain to the Council for Medical Schemes.
  6. Record the outcome, so the agent can track which schemes and codes cause disputes.
